Music and the law

What gets a channel muted, struck or banned, and how to never worry about it. Taught by Naomi, a lawyer.

Copyright: the person who made a song owns it. Playing it on your stream is a public performance and the platform's robots know every song. Twitch mutes VODs, YouTube strikes the channel, three strikes and the channel is gone.

Fair use is not a magic word. Reacting to a song, playing it quietly, playing only 30 seconds: none of these protect you. Only cleared music protects you.

Cleared for this school: the 88.5 Rock FM live stream as background on your Starting Soon and BRB scenes. Twitch Soundtrack, YouTube Audio Library and the platform's own music tools are cleared for everyone.

Games are usually fine to stream; publishers want it. Films, TV, sports and other people's streams are not. Reading the news is fine; reading a whole article aloud is not.

Other people's faces and voices: in public, generally fine; in private spaces, ask. Children are a separate rule set, covered in lesson 10. When a copyright claim arrives, do not panic and do not ignore it: remove the content, read the notice, ask in the cohort group.

Do it

  1. Replace every piece of uncleared music in your scenes.
  2. Turn off game music if the game uses licensed songs (Fortnite radio, sports games).
  3. Read your platform's copyright page once, slowly.
  4. Write down what you will do if a claim arrives.
  5. Bring one question to the Saturday Q&A.

Assignment

A stream using only cleared audio, with a one-line note in the group of what you changed.
